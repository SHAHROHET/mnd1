Key facts

  • If someone you are caring for chooses to die at home, they should first speak to their doctor or palliative care team to create an appropriate plan.
  • Encourage the person you are caring for to have all their legal documents in place.
  • After an expected death at home, call the person's doctor or palliative care team.
  • There are many resources for carers to help with funeral planning and other arrangements after someone has died.
  • Support is available to help carers to cope emotionally during the grieving process.

How do I prepare for a coming death at home?

Many people prefer to die at home in familiar surroundings. For those in end-of-life care, their home can provide a sense of freedom, peace and privacy.

You may be caring for someone at home who has a terminal condition and is dying. This article gives you advice on how to prepare for a death at home.

If the person you care for knows they are dying, they should talk to their doctor or palliative care team about how that might happen and what they want.

Their doctor or palliative care team may ask them:

  • Do they want to die at home?
  • Do they want to be resuscitated, if possible?
  • Are there other family members or friends who need to be informed?

They should either involve you in the discussion or tell you afterward, so you know what to expect.

Palliative Care Australia can help you find palliative care providers near you.

Caring for someone who is dying

Try to make the person you're caring for comfortable. Treat them with dignity and compassion. Try to keep them as free of pain as possible.

If they know they are dying, they might want to talk about their life. They might also want to be left in silence. Let them lead the way.

The person you are caring for may have medical problems. If they have severe pain or other symptoms, call the doctor or palliative care team for advice.

It's a good idea to arrange for someone to come to your home to support you during your loved one's final days or hours. This person may be a close relative, a friend or a nurse.

What legal documents do I need to prepare for a death at home?

Ideally, the person you care for will already have legal documents in place, such as those described below.

Death at home documents

Your doctor can help by providing you with 'death at home' documents, such as a letter telling the ambulance service the person is expected to die and should not be resuscitated.

A will

A will states how you want your belongings distributed after your death. Follow the link to find out more about wills and powers of attorney.

Enduring power of attorney

An enduring power of attorney states who can make legal and financial decisions for you if you can't do it yourself.

Appointing an enduring guardian

An enduring guardian is someone who can make decisions about your medical care and lifestyle if you can't do it yourself. An enduring guardian should be someone who you trust and know well. This person should be named in your enduring guardianship documents.

Find out more about guardianship and power of attorney.

Advance care planning

Advance care planning is the process of planning for your current and future health care.

Advance care plans are not legally binding. Your doctor can discuss advance care planning with you and your family.

Advance care directive

To formalise your advance care plan, you should write an advance care directive. This is also known as an advance health directive, or a living will.

The forms for writing advance care directives vary between states and territories. Follow the link to find out more about advance care planning in your state or territory (or call 1300 208 582 for advice).

What if these documents are not available?

If these arrangements are not in place for the person you are caring for, you can see if there is time to arrange them now. It is especially important to organise a will. Dying without a will can create problems and upset for family members.

What should I do after a death at home?

The death of a person you have been caring for can be difficult to deal with. It is natural to feel grief and a mix of emotions. Try to stay calm — reach out to a family member or friend if you would like support. There is no legal requirement for you to contact anyone straight away. You do not need to contact the ambulance or police for an expected home death.

When you are ready, call the person's doctor or palliative care team. They can arrange for a doctor to visit to confirm the person's death and issue a medical certificate of cause of death. If there is no doctor available, an authorised nurse or paramedic can complete a verification of death form.

If they die during the night you can wait until the morning before calling a doctor.

Read more on what to do when someone dies at home.

When is a death referred to the coroner?

If the doctor cannot confirm the cause of death, they will have to call the police. The police refer 'reportable deaths' to the coroner. These include when the person has died:

  • violently or unnaturally
  • unusually or in suspicious circumstances
  • unexpectedly
  • after an accident, injury or surgery

The coroner can ask for a post-mortem or autopsy to decide the cause of death.

What should I do in the days and weeks after a death at home?

First, be kind to yourself. You will probably be feeling a whole range of emotions and will find it hard to concentrate. Grieving can take a long time and each person's experience is unique. It is important to look after your own physical and mental health.

Get support from family and friends. If you feel comfortable, allow people to help if they offer.

Services Australia have payments and services that may help you after someone has died.

How do I arrange a funeral?

Call a funeral director to collect the person's body and arrange the funeral.

They will pass on the doctor's certificate, noting the cause of death, to the state or territory Registry of Births, Deaths and Marriages. The registry then issues a death certificate.

The funeral director will follow the wishes of the deceased person and their family. This includes whether they want to have a cremation or burial. Visit the Australian Funeral Directors Association page for a practical guide to the funeral process.

You may not want to use a funeral director. In this case, the person taking charge of the funeral must register the death. Visit the Services Australia page on births, deaths and marriages register in your state or territory.

Which organisations should I notify after a death?

After someone dies, you will need to notify many people and organisations, including:

  • their bank
  • their superannuation fund
  • the Australian Taxation Office

See the Services Australia page on what to do following a death for details of social and financial support.

Resources and support

  • Carer Gateway has information on end-of-life caring and support via 1800 422 737 to discuss your situation.
  • Griefline provides support online and on 1300 845 745.
  • Lifeline offers support on 13 11 14 and online about loss and grief.
  • The Compassionate Friends Australia offers support after the death of a child on 1300 064 068.
  • Cancer Council has information on facing end of life and what to do after death or via their support and information line on 13 11 20.
  • Dementia Australia can help with coping after the death of someone with dementia online or by calling 1800 100 500.

For help finding the right services, call the healthdirect helpline on 1800 022 222 (known as NURSE-ON-CALL in Victoria). A registered nurse is available 24 hours a day, 7 days a week.

If you need to speak to a doctor or mental health professional, use the Service Finder to find one nearest to you.
